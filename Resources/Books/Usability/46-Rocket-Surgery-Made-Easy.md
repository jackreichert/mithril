# Rocket Surgery Made Easy — Steve Krug (2009)

This companion to *Don't Make Me Think* turns usability testing into a routine that ordinary product teams can run without a laboratory or a large research budget. Krug deliberately reduces the method to its essential feedback loop: recruit a few people, watch them attempt realistic tasks, identify the most serious recurring problems, make focused fixes, and test again. The method trades statistical claims and exhaustive research for frequent qualitative evidence, making it especially useful during iterative product development when delayed learning is expensive.

## The case for testing

### Ch 1 — You Don't See Any Elephants Around Here, Do You?

Teams often skip usability testing because they imagine it must be expensive, formal, and performed by specialists near the end of a project. That belief protects design assumptions from contact with users and allows obvious problems to survive until they are costly to change. Lightweight testing is valuable because even a small number of observed sessions can reveal severe misunderstandings that the people building the product have become too familiar with it to notice.

### Ch 2 — I Will Now Saw My Lovely Assistant in Half

A usability test is not a demonstration of the product or an examination of the participant; it is an observation of how the design supports or obstructs a task. The facilitator must make that distinction explicit so participants feel free to struggle, criticize, and change direction without trying to please the team. When the session preserves this psychological safety, confusion becomes evidence about the interface rather than evidence about the person using it.

### Ch 3 — A Morning a Month, That's All We Ask

Krug proposes a recurring morning of tests each month because a modest cadence that actually happens is more valuable than an ideal study that remains unscheduled. Testing several participants in succession, debriefing immediately, and choosing fixes the same day compresses the learning loop. A fixed rhythm also changes team behavior: people design with the next observation session in mind and become less attached to unsupported assumptions.

## Getting ready

### Ch 4 — What Do You Test, and When Do You Test It?

Teams can test almost any representation that lets a participant attempt a meaningful task, including sketches, wireframes, prototypes, competitor products, and functioning software. Testing early answers structural questions before implementation hardens them, while testing later exposes problems in content, interaction, performance, and integration. The artifact's fidelity should match the question being asked so participants do not mistake unfinished details for the subject of the study.

### Ch 5 — Recruit Loosely and Grade on a Curve

Participants should resemble the intended audience enough to encounter the product's important assumptions, but perfect demographic matching is often unnecessary for finding broad usability failures. Loose recruiting lowers the cost of frequent testing and is preferable to postponing all observation while searching for ideal participants. Domain expertise, accessibility needs, language, risk, or specialized workflows can make closer matching essential, so the recruiting standard should tighten when those characteristics materially affect the task.

### Ch 6 — Find Some Things for Them to Do

Good tasks give participants a realistic goal and enough context to act without telling them which controls to use. Tasks should exercise important or uncertain workflows, avoid clues copied from interface labels, and have an observable completion state. A short scenario makes the motivation credible, but excessive backstory adds noise and can turn the session into a reading exercise rather than an interaction test.

### Ch 7 — Some Boring Checklists

Reliable lightweight testing still depends on preparation: confirm the artifact, equipment, recording consent, observers, schedule, tasks, and backup plans before participants arrive. Checklists prevent small logistical failures from consuming the limited session or distracting the facilitator from behavior. The process should remain proportional to the study, using enough structure to protect the evidence without recreating the bureaucracy the lightweight method is meant to avoid.

## Conducting the session

### Ch 8 — Mind Reading Made Easy

Think-aloud facilitation asks participants to describe what they notice, expect, and believe while attempting a task. The facilitator uses neutral prompts and avoids teaching, defending, or signaling the correct path because assistance changes the behavior under observation. Silence and mistakes are useful data, and follow-up questions should clarify the participant's mental model without turning the session into an interview detached from actual use.

### Ch 9 — Make It a Spectator Sport

Broadcasting sessions to a nearby team room lets designers, developers, writers, and stakeholders observe the same behavior directly. Shared observation creates empathy and reduces arguments based on secondhand summaries, especially when observers record problems rather than proposing fixes during the session. Participant privacy and consent remain mandatory, and the audience must avoid interruptions or reactions that make the participant feel judged.

## Making use of what you learn

### Ch 10 — Debriefing 101

The team should debrief soon after the sessions while observations remain fresh and everyone shares the same evidence. Rather than compiling every comment, the group identifies the most serious problems that appeared repeatedly or blocked important tasks. A focused debrief separates observed behavior from interpretation, ranks impact, and produces a short list of changes the team can realistically make before the next round.

### Ch 11 — The Least You Can Do

Usability problems do not always require redesigning the entire interface; the smallest change that removes the observed confusion is often the best next experiment. Clarifying a label, strengthening hierarchy, removing a distraction, or exposing a hidden action can restore the user's path with less risk than a broad redesign. The change should address the underlying cause rather than merely decorate the symptom, and the next test determines whether it actually helped.

### Ch 12 — The Usual Suspects

Certain problems recur across products: unclear purpose, weak visual hierarchy, misleading labels, hidden navigation, excessive content, and controls that do not look or behave as expected. Recognizing these patterns helps teams diagnose observations quickly, but the pattern catalog should not replace watching what participants actually do. A familiar heuristic becomes actionable only when connected to a concrete task failure and an interface change that can be tested.

### Ch 13 — Making Sure Life Actually Improves

Research has no product value when findings remain in a report while the same problems ship unchanged. Teams need explicit ownership, a small prioritized fix list, and a near-term opportunity to retest the affected tasks. Closing the loop also means preserving important findings where future decisions can use them without turning the repository into an unranked archive of observations.

### Ch 14 — Teleportation Made Easy

Remote testing lets teams reach participants who cannot share a physical room and can reduce scheduling and travel friction. Screen sharing and conferencing preserve much of the think-aloud method, but technical setup, privacy, latency, device context, and the facilitator's reduced view of body language require extra care. Modern tools make remote research routine, yet the same principles hold: realistic tasks, neutral facilitation, consent, attentive observation, and prompt follow-through.

## Enduring contribution and cautions

The book's enduring contribution is procedural: it makes direct observation cheap enough to become a habit rather than a milestone. Three qualitative sessions can expose major breakdowns, but they do not establish prevalence, compare variants statistically, or represent every audience segment, so teams must avoid turning vivid anecdotes into population claims. High-risk domains, accessibility research, international audiences, sensitive data, and consequential decisions may require specialist research methods, stricter recruiting, or formal ethics and privacy review in addition to Krug's lightweight loop.

## Why it belongs in Mithril

- **Process:** provides a repeatable observe-prioritize-fix-retest cadence that fits iterative delivery.
- **Usability review:** grounds findings in task breakdowns witnessed with representative users rather than reviewer preference alone.
- **Specification:** turns important workflows into realistic research tasks with observable completion states.
- **Evidence limits:** distinguishes fast qualitative diagnosis from statistical validation and calls for stronger methods when risk or audience differences demand them.
